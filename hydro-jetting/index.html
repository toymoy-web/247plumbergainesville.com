Hydro Jetting in Gainesville, FL

Hydro jetting cleans a drain or sewer line with a high-pressure stream of water pushed through a specialized nozzle. A cable machine punches a hole through a clog. Jetting scrubs the whole inside wall of the pipe, taking grease, roots and scale with it.

It's the step after snaking stops working. If sewage is backing up right now, the line is answered 24/7.

Hydro jetting at a glance

  • Clears grease, roots, scale and sludge, not just a channel.
  • Camera check before jetting older or fragile pipe.
  • Residential sewer laterals and commercial kitchen lines.
  • Backups answered 24/7.

Snaked it and it clogged again a few weeks later?

That pattern is the most common reason people end up asking about jetting. These are the situations where it usually earns its cost.

  • !
    The same line keeps clogging after cabling. The cable opened a path, but the buildup on the pipe wall stayed behind.
  • !
    A cable got stuck partway down. Heavy roots or packed grease can stop a cable that a jetter can cut through.
  • !
    Roots showed up on a camera. Jetting cuts root masses back to the pipe wall.
  • !
    A kitchen line that handles heavy cooking. Grease hardens into a layer that only jetting really removes.
  • !
    Slow drains throughout a house with cast iron pipe. Rust scale roughens the pipe and catches everything.

Snaking vs. hydro jetting

Drain cable and hydro jetting compared.
FactorCable (snake)Hydro jetting
What it does.Bores a hole through the clog.Scours the full pipe wall.
Best for.A single soft clog, a toilet or sink trap.Grease, roots, scale and repeat clogs.
How long it lasts.Until buildup closes the hole again.Much longer, since the buildup is gone.
Risk to old pipe.Low.Needs a camera check first on fragile pipe.

Neither is better in every case. A first clog in a healthy line usually just needs main drain cleaning with a cable. Jetting is the answer when the same line keeps coming back.

When jetting is the wrong tool

Pressure that clears grease can also finish off a pipe that is already failing. Some older Gainesville laterals are clay tile with open joints. Others are cast iron that has rusted thin, or fiber pipe from the 1950s and 60s that collapses when it's disturbed.

That's why older lines get a full-length sewer camera inspection before any jetting. If the camera shows a collapse, a break or a pipe that's barely holding together, jetting stops and main sewer line repair becomes the conversation.

Restaurant lines downtown, in Midtown near UF and around Butler Plaza collect grease fast, and hydro jetting is the standard fix there. In older neighborhoods like the Duckpond and Pleasant Street, it clears scale and roots out of cast iron and clay pipe that a cable would only punch a hole through.

How a jetting job runs

  1. 1
    Camera first. We look at the line's condition and find the problem spots before any pressure goes in.
  2. 2
    Access from the cleanout. The jetting hose goes in from the outdoor cleanout, away from living space.
  3. 3
    Choose the nozzle. A root-cutting head for roots, a scouring head for grease and scale.
  4. 4
    Work the line in passes. The rear-facing jets pull the hose forward while flushing debris back toward the main.
  5. 5
    Camera again. A second look confirms the line is clear and shows anything left to watch.

Restaurant and commercial kitchen lines

Commercial kitchens send far more grease down the drain than any home, even with a working grease interceptor. GRU runs an oil and grease management program for businesses on its sewer system, and keeping kitchen lines clean is part of staying on the right side of it.

Scheduled jetting, done after closing, keeps a kitchen line from backing up during a dinner rush. Our business commercial plumbing service covers the interceptor side of the job.

We jet lines across Gainesville and in Alachua, where both homes and businesses tie into the same kinds of aging laterals.

GRU oil and grease management program for commercial wastewater customers. Drain cleaning practice under the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, based on the International Plumbing Code (IPC).
