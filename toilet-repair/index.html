Toilet Repair & Installation · Gainesville, FL

Emergency Toilet Repair and Installation in Gainesville, FL

Toilet repair covers the handful of parts that fail in every toilet eventually: the flapper, the fill valve, the wax ring under the bowl and the supply line behind it. Installation is the full swap, old toilet out and new one set, sealed and tested.

Most toilet problems are not emergencies. An overflowing bowl or water seeping across the floor is, and our line is answered 24/7 for exactly that.

Emergency toilet repair: overflowing, backing up or leaking

An overflowing toilet is an emergency the moment water reaches the floor. So is a toilet that backs up into a tub, a cracked bowl that leaks, or a supply line that has let go and is spraying the wall.

Most of these stop in seconds if you know where to close the valve. The oval shutoff on the wall behind the toilet is the one to know, and if it will not turn, the main is the fallback.

In the first minute

  1. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper closed, or turn the wall valve clockwise.
  2. If the valve will not turn, do not force it. Close the main shutoff instead.
  3. Do not flush again, and do not plunge if the bowl is full of water that is not draining.
  4. Tell us if other drains are gurgling, since that points to the main line.

A running toilet costs more in Gainesville than you would think

A toilet that runs all day can send hundreds of gallons down the drain without anyone noticing. GRU bills wastewater based on the water that passes through your meter, so every gallon a running toilet wastes gets charged again on the sewer side.

That is why a hissing tank is worth fixing this week rather than someday. A flapper or fill valve is a small part. A few months of doubled charges is not.

If the toilet is quiet but the bill is still high, the leak is somewhere else. Our leak detection service can check the rest of the system.

Hard water and toilet parts

Gainesville's water runs about 8.2 grains per gallon hard. Over a few years that mineral content stiffens rubber flappers, crusts the fill valve and narrows the small rim holes that rinse the bowl. The result is a toilet that runs, refills slowly or flushes weakly, even though nothing is broken.

When we replace parts, we use fill valves and flappers that tolerate mineral buildup well, and we clear the rim jets while the tank is open. It adds a few minutes and usually restores the flush.

Repair or replace: how we decide with you

Most toilets are worth repairing. The china bowl lasts decades, and the parts that wear out are inexpensive and standard.

Replacement makes sense in a few specific cases. A cracked tank or bowl cannot be repaired safely. A toilet made before 1994 may use 3.5 gallons or more per flush, compared with 1.28 gallons for a WaterSense-labeled model today. In a GRU home, where water and wastewater are both billed on usage, that difference adds up month after month.

Other reasons people replace: a taller bowl that is easier to use, a toilet that has clogged every month for years, or a bathroom remodel. We will tell you if a repair will do the job.

Before you call about a toilet

  • ✓

  • ✓
    Do not keep flushing a clogged toilet. One more flush is how bathrooms flood.
  • ✓

  • ✓
    Note the toilet's age. A date is often stamped inside the tank lid. It helps us decide between repair and replacement.

When more than one fixture backs up at once, the toilet is just where the water shows up. That is a job for main drain cleaning on the main line.

What a new toilet installation includes

  1. 1
    Remove the old toilet. Drain it, disconnect it and lift it clear of the flange.
  2. 2
    Inspect the flange. A cracked or rusted flange is repaired before anything new goes down, since it is the most common cause of future base leaks.
  3. 3
    Set on a new seal. New wax ring or wax-free seal, new closet bolts and a new supply line.
  4. 4
    Level, secure and caulk. No rocking, no gaps.
  5. 5
    Test. Several flushes, a check for leaks at the base and the tank, and an adjusted fill level.

Toilets in older Gainesville houses

Homes in the Duckpond and other pre-war neighborhoods often sit on raised foundations, with cast iron drain pipe under the floor. The flange that holds the toilet down in these houses is often cast iron too, and after decades it can rust thin or crack when the bolts are tightened.

Older bathrooms also sometimes have a rough-in, the distance from the wall to the drain, that is not the modern 12-inch standard. We measure before you buy a new toilet, so it actually fits the drain where it sits. If the floor under the old toilet has gone soft from a slow leak, that gets addressed before a new one goes down.

Toilets on septic, city sewer and in rentals

Most Gainesville homes are on GRU sewer. Farther out, in places like Archer, many homes run on septic tanks, where a constantly running toilet can flood the drain field. On septic, a running toilet is worth fixing the same day.

Landlords and property managers with units near the University of Florida can book toilet repairs directly, and a leaking wax ring in an upstairs unit is one of the calls worth making before the ceiling below shows a stain. If the same bathroom also has a dripping faucet, faucet and sink repair can happen on the same visit while the water is off.

Sources: EPA WaterSense (1.28 gallons per flush labeling threshold), GRU customer service (wastewater billed on metered water use), GRU water quality FAQ (hardness).

Wax rings, flush levers and installation quotes

The wax ring, the flush lever, the fill valve and the flapper cover most toilet repairs. A leak at the base points to the wax ring, and a handle that sticks or hangs points to the lever.

If you want a new toilet, ask for a price that includes removing the old one. Running toilets in more than one bathroom are common in older homes and are worth fixing together so the water bill drops.
