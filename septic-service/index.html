Emergency septic service: backups, alarms and soggy yards

A septic system tolerates a lot until it does not. Slow drains throughout the house, a sewage smell outdoors, a wet patch over the drain field and an alarm on the control panel are all calls to make today, not next week.

Alachua County's own septic page notes that conventional systems are not designed to remove nitrogen, and parts of the county drain toward creeks and springs. A failing system is worth treating as urgent for the property and for the water around it.

Until we arrive

  1. Cut water use. Skip laundry, long showers and the dishwasher.
  2. Keep the tank lid closed, since the gases inside are hazardous.
  3. Note whether an alarm is sounding and where the ground is wet.
  4. Keep people and pets off the soggy area.

How a septic system works

Wastewater leaves the house through a buried line into the septic tank. Solids settle to the bottom as sludge, grease floats to the top as scum, and the liquid in the middle flows out to the drain field, where it soaks into the soil.

The tank only works if it's pumped before the sludge and scum layers get too thick. Once they do, solids wash out into the drain field and clog it. A clogged drain field is the expensive failure. A pumped tank is the cheap prevention.

The EPA's general guidance is to have a household septic tank inspected every few years and pumped typically every three to five years. The right interval depends on tank size, household size and habits like using a garbage disposal, which adds solids.

Inside the city, most homes are on GRU sewer, so septic calls come mostly from the unincorporated areas around it: rural lots off Millhopper Road in the northwest, the countryside toward Monteocha in the northeast, and acreage west toward Jonesville and Archer.

The line between the house and tank is plumbing, and it's often the simplest fix. A camera inspection shows whether it's cracked, root-filled or sagging, and underground sewer line repair handles the damage.

Septic systems near the springs

Alachua County has some of Florida's best-known springs along the Santa Fe River, near High Springs. Septic systems add nitrogen to groundwater, and in parts of the springs region, state springs-protection plans can require nitrogen-reducing systems when a septic system is new or replaced.

Septic permits in Florida are overseen by the Department of Environmental Protection. If your system needs more than a repair, check what applies to your specific property before choosing a replacement. Homes near the Santa Fe River, around High Springs, are worth checking first.

Keeping a septic system healthy

  1. 1
    Pump on schedule. Keep a record of the date and the sludge level at each pumping.
  2. 2
    Keep grease and wipes out. Even wipes labeled flushable don't break down in a tank.
  3. 3
    Fix running toilets fast. A running toilet can send hundreds of gallons a day into the drain field.
  4. 4
    Keep traffic off the drain field. No cars, sheds or pools over it.
  5. 5
    Spread out laundry. Several loads in a row can overload the field.
